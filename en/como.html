How Nexus Terminal works

What we do, how we read the market and what everything you see on the site means, explained without jargon. If you are new to trading, start here.

What Nexus Terminal does

Every day we analyse the latest close of thousands of US stocks, the main companies of Madrid, London, Germany, France and Tokyo, cryptocurrencies and currency pairs. We show you what each chart says, calculate forecasts for the next open and measure in public whether we are right. The data is from the latest close, not live.

Trading basics

Session, open and close
A session is an exchange's trading day. The open is the first price of the day and the close is the last. Each exchange has its own hours: Tokyo opens first, then Europe and finally New York.
Opening gap
The difference between yesterday's close and today's open. If an exchange opens above where it closed, there is a gap up.
Bullish and bearish
An asset is in an uptrend (bullish) when it generally rises over time; bearish when it falls. If it does neither, we talk about a range or sideways trend.
LONG and SHORT
LONG is betting that the price rises (buying). SHORT is betting that it falls (short selling). A SHORT carries different risks and requirements than buying, and not every broker allows it.
Volatility
How much an asset moves on a normal day. The more it moves, the more you can gain and the more you can lose.
Stop
An exit price you set in advance to limit the loss if the market goes against you. We suggest one based on volatility, but the decision is yours.

How we read a chart

Trend

20- and 50-session moving average

A moving average is the average price of the last 20 (or 50) sessions. If the 20 is above the 50, the price has been rising: uptrend. If it is below, downtrend. The "strength" is the distance between the two.

RSI

Has it gone too fast?

A number from 0 to 100 measuring the speed of the last 14 sessions. Above 70 is called overbought (it may pull back); below 30, oversold (it may bounce). We avoid signals when it is at an extreme.

ATR

The normal daily move

The average range an asset covers in a session. It tells you whether a move is normal or unusual and suggests a reasonable stop (1.5 times the ATR).

Highs and lows

Recent ceilings and floors

We look at the highest and lowest price of the last 20 sessions. An asset near its high in an uptrend is a classic setup; near its low in a downtrend, the opposite.

What a signal is

LONG: uptrend, RSI without an extreme overbought reading (below 68) and price within 3% of its 20-session high.
SHORT: downtrend, RSI without an extreme oversold reading (above 32) and price within 3% of its 20-session low.

A signal is not an order or a promise: it is a specific technical situation, measured with fixed rules applied equally to every asset. That is why we can honestly check afterwards how many times it worked. For each one we give an indicative horizon (1 to 10 sessions) based on its strength and volatility.

Forecasts for the next open

For each asset we search the history for similar situations (same trend, same RSI, same volatility) and look at what happened next. With that we give a probability and a probable range of movement. Then we correct it with what actually worked in the past, so that "60%" means something.

In the Scanner, the "Forecasts · date" tab is the next session, the one that has not opened yet. The other tabs are signals from days that have already passed, kept to measure results.

The measurement result, with the hit rate against chance, is always visible on the Forecasts page. If it does not beat chance yet, we say so.

The chain of markets

Markets move in a chain because the world never sleeps: Tokyo opens first, then Europe and finally New York, and what one does influences the next. We measure it with real data:

  • Tokyo → Madrid, London, Germany and France (same day): in the history, the open of each European exchange followed Tokyo's direction between 63% and 67% of days. On days with a strong move, considerably more.
  • New York → Tokyo (next day): what the US does into the close shows in Tokyo's open.
Important: the edge is mostly in how the market opens, not in what it does afterwards. From open to close, the hit rate is almost that of chance. That is why we talk about an "opening forecast" and do not promise profits.

Engine 2: a mix of signals with measured reliability

Engine 2 combines several real signals into a single probability: the chain of markets, the momentum of the last 5 days, the volatility of the last 20 sessions and the calendar (day of the week, turn of the month). It uses nothing that cannot be measured.

How it is checked: the model is trained only on what came before each date and then predicts days it has never seen ("walk-forward" validation). That measures, for each confidence level, how many days it was really right.

What we publish: only forecasts whose "safe floor" (see below) beats "always saying the same thing". If on a given day there are none, the message says so and forces nothing. If a market the engine needs is missing its latest close, it calculates nothing.

How to read it: "at this confidence (70% or more) it hit 79% of 211 days (safe floor 73%)" means that, in the past, when the engine was this sure, it got the direction right on 79% of 211 days it had not seen.

How to read reliability: the "safe floor"

With few days, a high hit rate can be luck. The "safe floor" is the reasonable minimum: with 95% statistical confidence, the real hit rate should not be lower than that figure. The more days measured, the closer it gets to the observed hit rate.

Example: being right 9 times out of 10 sounds great, but with so few days the safe floor would be low. Being right 160 out of 211 (76%) has a much higher safe floor, because there is more data.

Always compare the hit rate with "always saying the same thing": if a market rises 58% of days, guessing "up" every time is already right 58% of the time. Only what clearly beats that is worth it.

When it updates and where the data comes from

  • The Scanner updates every night with the previous day's close. Forecasts are calculated every morning (around 8:30 Spanish time) and a single message is sent to Telegram.
  • US stocks, crypto and forex: daily data from Polygon.io (Massive). Madrid, London, Germany, France and Tokyo: each exchange's daily closes downloaded from Yahoo Finance. It is an unofficial source and does not guarantee accuracy.
  • Before calculating, the robot checks that it has the latest close of every market it uses. If one is missing, that forecast is not published.
  • Sessions that have already closed are not sent: a forecast is only valid while its session has not started to pass.

Frequently asked questions

Is the data live?

No. It is from the latest close. This is an analysis and preparation tool, not a trading terminal.

Does it tell me what to buy and when to sell?

No. It shows you the technical situation of each asset and what has historically happened in similar situations. The decision and the risk are yours.

Does it guarantee results?

No. Nobody can. We measure how many times it was right in the past and show it as it is, misses included. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

Why is there sometimes no forecast for a market?

Because some recent data is missing, because the session has already closed or because the measured reliability does not clearly beat chance. We prefer not to publish rather than publish something weak.

What does "no proven edge" mean?

That, with the data measured so far, the forecast's hit rate is not yet statistically distinguishable from chance. We say it instead of hiding it.
